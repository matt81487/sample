## 1. War powers (Art. I §8 vs. Art. II)
- Congress declares war (Art. I §8); the President is Commander in Chief (Art. II). Hamilton: CinC = "nothing more than the supreme command and direction of the military and naval force." Biden's summary: **Congress decides WHETHER to make war; the President decides HOW.**
- **War Powers Resolution (1973):** passed over Nixon's veto at the end of Vietnam; class view: "not particularly successful."
  - **§1544(c) / §5(c):** concurrent resolution directing removal of forces = legislating without presentment → conflicts with **INS v. Chadha** (law needs bicameralism + presentment).
  - Congress's fallback: **power of the purse** (cut off funding), but that needs passage by both houses (and, for a law, presentment).
  - Class questions: is the 60–90 day clock an *approval* for 60–90 days? The WPR says it **does not give the President new authority**.
  - **§1541(c):** lists when the President may introduce forces (declaration, specific statutory authorization, or national emergency from an attack on the U.S.); class noted rescue operations have been proposed as an addition.
- **First Gulf War (1990–91): Bush vs. Sen. Biden.**
  - Bush: doesn't think he needs a congressional resolution; has the authority to implement UN resolutions; "many attorneys having so advised me"; force used 200+ times, few declarations of war; has consulted Congress extensively.
  - Biden: Federalist Papers say the war power "appertains to the legislature"; James Wilson: no "single man" can involve us in war; 400,000 troops = war, so it needs clear, unambiguous authorization (not necessarily a formal declaration). Rebuts the "200 precedents" argument: most weren't against sovereign nations, were minor police/rescue actions, were responses to sudden attacks, or were congressionally authorized (Gulf of Tonkin). Example: 1824 pirate landing in Spanish Cuba. Jefferson: Congress "alone" can change "our condition from peace to war." Buchanan: without Congress, the President can't "fire a hostile gun... except to repel attacks."
- **Obama Q&A (Savage, Boston Globe, 12/20/2007):** the President lacks constitutional power to unilaterally authorize an attack that doesn't involve stopping an **actual or imminent threat**; self-defense is allowed before consulting Congress; it's preferable to have Congress's informed consent. S.J. Res. 23: offensive action against Iran must be explicitly authorized.

## 2. Other federalism: Supremacy Clause and preemption
- Any valid federal law preempts inconsistent state law (Art. VI cl. 2).
- **Three kinds of preemption (Murphy v. NCAA, as presented in class):** (1) **conflict**: federal and state law inconsistent, federal wins; (2) **express**: the statute says it preempts; (3) **field**: federal law so occupies the field that there's no room for states.
- **17th Amendment** (direct election of senators): discussed as "a blow to federalism?"

## 3. Anti-commandeering
- **New York v. United States (1992):** Congress can't compel states to enact or enforce a federal regulatory program ("take title" provision). "Where a federal interest is sufficiently strong... it must [legislate] directly; it may not conscript state governments as its agents." The Constitution "confers upon Congress the power to regulate individuals, not States" (a break from the Articles; Federalist 15's "great and radical vice").
- **Allowed alternatives:** conditional spending; **conditional preemption**: Congress may offer states the choice of regulating by federal standards or having state law preempted.
- **Printz v. United States (1997):** the Brady Act required local **chief law enforcement officers (CLEOs)** to do background checks. Holding: Congress can't circumvent New York "by conscripting the State's officers directly"; it applies even to mundane, non-policymaking tasks.
  - **State courts CAN be required to enforce federal law** (Supremacy Clause text; **Testa v. Katt**). **State legislatures: NO. State executive officers: NO.** (Class chart: courts yes / legislatures no / executive no.)
  - "States are not mere political subdivisions... neither regional offices nor administrative agencies of the Federal Government."
  - **Separation-of-powers point:** the Take Care Clause (Art. II §3) puts execution of federal law in the President; commandeering CLEOs transfers that duty to thousands of officers outside presidential control. Unity in the Executive (Federalist 70).
  - The **Necessary and Proper** answer: a law violating state sovereignty isn't "proper" (Federalist 33: "merely [an] act of usurpation"). Federalism protections come from many provisions, not only the 10th Amendment ("states but a truism," Darby).
  - The first Congresses gave state courts jurisdiction to enforce federal civil and criminal laws (historical practice cited).
  - **Federalist 44 (Madison):** state officers take an oath to support the federal Constitution because they have an "essential agency in giving effect to" it (the federal government has no role in enforcing state constitutions).
  - **Federalist 51:** "double security": division between federal and state plus separation among the federal branches.
  - Stevens dissent: political safeguards (see the earlier Printz quote).
- **Reno v. Condon (2000), 9–0, called "corrective of Printz" in class:** the **Driver's Privacy Protection Act (DPPA)** restricts states' disclosure and sale of drivers' personal information. It's valid under the Commerce Clause (the information is a "thing in interstate commerce"). It doesn't require states to regulate their own citizens; it regulates the **states as owners of databases**. Governed by **South Carolina v. Baker** (a law that "regulated state activities" rather than "seeking to control or influence the manner in which States regulate private parties"). Compliance burdens on state employees don't make it commandeering.
- **Murphy v. NCAA (2018) (Alito):** the most recent summary of anti-commandeering. The doctrine = the Constitution withholds from Congress "the power to issue orders directly to the States"; **dual sovereignty**, with states retaining "a residuary and inviolable sovereignty" (Federalist 39). **Three reasons for the rule:** (1) a structural protection of **liberty** (divided power protects individuals); (2) **political accountability** (voters know whom to credit or blame); (3) prevents Congress from **shifting the costs** of regulation to the states.
- **Art. I §10:** what states can't do (no treaties, letters of marque, coining money, bills of attainder, ex post facto laws, etc.).

## 4. Individual rights: federalism and the Bill of Rights
- **Barron v. Baltimore (1833) (Marshall):** the **Bill of Rights (here, the 5th Amendment Takings Clause) restricts only the FEDERAL government**, not the states.
  - Reasoning: the Constitution was established by the people "for themselves, for their own government, and not for the government of the individual states." When the framers meant to restrict states, they said so expressly (**Art. I §10**: "No state shall..."), while Art. I §9's limits (e.g., bill of attainder/ex post facto) apply only to the federal government despite general wording. The amendments came from fears of the *general* government, and contain no language applying them to states. Result: no jurisdiction; dismissed.
  - Pre-1868 (before the 14th Amendment); later incorporation runs through the 14th Amendment.
  - First Amendment text: "**Congress** shall make no law..." (textual support for federal-only application).
