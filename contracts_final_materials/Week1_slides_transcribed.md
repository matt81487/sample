## The Record Deal (R89(a))
Drake agreed to work for Columbia Records at $10K per week for 1 year, beginning April 1, under a written contract. Thereafter, RCA Records offered Drake $11K per week, to the shock of Drake and Columbia. Drake and Columbia thereafter agreed that Columbia would pay Drake $11K a week. They tore up the original contract and, simultaneously, executed a new contract on March 15 for $11K per week. Is the March 15 contract enforceable?
A. No, it lacked consideration. B. No, because Drake had a preexisting duty to perform for RCA. C. Yes, because Drake promised additional consideration. D. Yes. E. A & B.
**Answer: D.** R89(a): a promise modifying an executory contract is binding if the modification is "fair and equitable in view of circumstances not anticipated by the parties when the contract was made." RCA's unexpected higher offer was an unanticipated circumstance; Columbia voluntarily agreed to the raise; no coercion, bad faith or improper threat by Drake. Enforceable under §89 even though Drake was already obligated to work for Columbia.

## The Application (preexisting duty / new consideration)
Anoki hires Amira to type his 40-page PhD application within seven days for $2,000. On day 5 they discover the last 20 pages must be typed in Mandarin, which neither knew when contracting. Anoki doesn't speak Mandarin; Amira is fluent and can easily translate and type it. Having finished 50%, Amira says she won't finish at the original price unless Anoki pays $500 more (the reasonable value of the labor). Knowing it's too late to find another typist, Anoki reluctantly agrees. On delivery he pays only $2,000. Amira sues for $500.
A. Anoki prevails because the modification lacked consideration. B. Amira wins because the modification is valid. C. Amira wins because Anoki promised more money. D. Anoki wins because a preexisting contractual duty can never be modified after performance has begun.
**Answer: B.** Common law: a modification generally needs new consideration (preexisting duty rule); a promise to pay more for the *same* performance is unenforceable unless the party provides additional or different performance. Unforeseen circumstance: neither knew half had to be in Mandarin, a material change in the nature of the work. Change in performance / new consideration: translating into Mandarin goes *beyond* the original obligation to type, so it supplies new consideration. (Class notes: "This is tested every year"; only 35-50% get it right.)

## Momo v. Berwyn (use Joyner & Frigaliment)
Momo, Inc. agrees to sell, and Berwyn, Inc. agrees to buy, 500 "chicken." Momo delivers stewing chickens; Berwyn rejects, saying "chicken" means frying chicken. Each claims breach. At trial, Berwyn's witness testifies Berwyn's lawyer didn't draft the "chicken" portion. The trial court finds the term ambiguous, the parties of equal bargaining power, and that both drafted most of the contract. Because of the testimony, the judge construes the term against Momo (Momo chose the word, likely had reason to know of its uncertainty, and could protect itself). Momo appeals. Who is likely to prevail?
(Analysis slide not captured. Per Joyner and the Week 1 notes: construing against the drafter is a last resort and inappropriate between sophisticated parties of equal bargaining power who both drafted; the court should determine intended meaning (R201), and under Frigaliment the party urging the narrower meaning (Berwyn, "frying") bears the burden. Likely result: reversed/remanded; Momo prevails on appeal.)

## Sally's Olive Oil? (R201)
Steve agreed to buy, and Sally to sell, all barrels of Sally's Olive Oil arriving on the ship "Atlantic Courtyard" on May 13 at 2:00 pm. There are two ships of that name arriving then, with significantly different quantities, in different cities. Steve meant New York; Sally meant New Jersey. Putting aside the evidence, what are the potential outcomes?
**Analysis (R201):**
- Steve's meaning controls if Sally knew or had reason to know Steve meant New York, while Steve didn't know or have reason to know Sally meant New Jersey.
- Sally's meaning controls if Steve knew or had reason to know Sally meant New Jersey, while Sally didn't know or have reason to know Steve meant New York.
- No agreement on the disputed term if neither knew or had reason to know of the other's meaning, and the ship's identity materially affects the quantity: no mutual assent, no enforceable contract. Same outcome if BOTH knew of the ambiguity.

## Sherrodd v. Morrison: Minimum Learning Objectives (R213, R214)
- Understand the fraud exception to the Parol Evidence Rule.
- Fraud generally may permit extrinsic evidence despite an integrated writing. But under Sherrodd a party may not use the fraud exception to introduce an alleged prior representation that directly contradicts express terms of the writing on the same subject. Sherrodd relies on fraud in the inducement (R164).
- Sherrodd claimed reliance on representations about excavation amount and compensation, but the contract said Sherrodd had satisfied itself regarding the "character, quantity, and kind of materials to be encountered," and contained a merger clause; the oral representations directly contradicted the writing.
- A merger clause and specific written terms matter in deciding whether alleged reliance on a prior representation is legally sustainable.
- Key issue: is the alleged fraudulent representation independent of the written terms, or would it vary/contradict them? Fraud in the execution (R163) is different: it challenges the validity or nature of the writing itself.

## The Tree Contract (R214, R163 vs. R164)
Tony and Chris orally agree Tony will buy one Christmas tree for $100. Chris says Tony must sign a document acknowledging receipt of the $100, and presents it so Tony can't reasonably discover its contents. Tony reasonably believes it's a receipt and signs. It's actually a contract requiring Tony to buy five more trees, a term Chris deliberately concealed. Tony offers Chris's prior statements to show he didn't assent; Chris says they contradict the writing. Does the PER exclude the evidence?
**Answer: No.** R214: the PER bars prior/contemporaneous agreements contradicting a fully integrated writing, but not evidence offered to show illegality, fraud, duress, mistake, lack of consideration, or another invalidating cause. Chris tricked Tony into believing he signed a receipt: fraud in the execution (R163). Tony may introduce extrinsic evidence that his apparent assent was ineffective.
- Fraud in the execution (R163): "I was deceived about what I was signing." (Assent generally ineffective.)
- Fraud in the inducement (R164): "I knew what I was signing, but lies induced me to sign it." (Ordinarily voidable, but can't contradict direct terms.)

## Rum Barrels (shared meaning vs. statute)
Isabel agrees to sell rum to Binny's Liquor Store at $2,000 a barrel. When signing, both parties and others in their trade use standard wooden barrels holding 30 gallons. A local statute defines a "barrel" as a 33-gallon container. Does the statute prevent interpreting the agreement as the 30-gallon barrels in use?
**Answer: No.** The parties' shared understanding ordinarily controls. Isabel, Binny's and the trade all understood "barrel" as the 30-gallon barrel actually used; the statutory definition doesn't by itself override that mutually understood contractual meaning.

## The Mali Purchase (R201: shared meaning beats reasonable-person reading)
LeBron and King Richards contract for LeBron's purchase of The Mali, a beachfront property in Senegal. A provision requires "twelve equal payments over the course of twelve months totaling not less than $144 million U.S. Dollars." A court determines a reasonable person would read it as $12 million per month. Each party actually understood it to require 24 equal payments within 2 years. What will a court do?
**Answer:** Enforce the shared meaning: 24 equal payments over two years (R201). When both parties attach the same meaning, it controls, even though it conflicts with the objectively reasonable reading. Objective interpretation matters mainly when the parties attached DIFFERENT meanings.

## Taylor v. State Farm: Minimum Learning Objectives (R215, 214, 212, 200)
Know the Restrictive View vs. the Corbin View on extrinsic evidence to interpret a contract.
- **Restrictive / plain-meaning view:** the judge first examines the writing; extrinsic evidence only if the language is ambiguous on its face; if clear, outside evidence is excluded. Greater weight to the objective meaning of the words.
- **Corbin view (adopted by the Arizona Supreme Court in Taylor):** the judge may first (provisionally) consider the offered extrinsic evidence to decide whether the language is "reasonably susceptible" to the proponent's interpretation; no finding of facial ambiguity required. If reasonably susceptible, the evidence is admitted to determine intended meaning. Extrinsic evidence may INTERPRET, but not simply vary or contradict, the agreement.

## The Car Payment (PER: later agreements) = Finals Prep Q5
Hamilton and Walker sign a completely integrated written agreement (May 25) for Hamilton to buy 100 cars for $1M, delivery June 6. In a payment dispute, Hamilton offers evidence that on May 30 they orally agreed payment could be $800K cash and $200K in promissory notes. Walker denies it. Does the PER bar the evidence?
**Answer: No.** The PER bars prior or contemporaneous agreements that contradict or supplement a fully integrated writing. It does not apply to agreements made AFTER execution. The May 30 agreement is a subsequent modification and isn't barred by the PER.

## David & Goliath (substantial performance; diminution in value)
David agrees to build Goliath a house for $300,000. The contract expressly requires foam insulation. David unintentionally installs cotton insulation, reducing market value by $6,000. Replacing it would cost $198,000 (reopening walls). The court finds David believed he was using foam because the manufacturer negligently mislabeled it. David says good faith means no breach and he owes nothing; Goliath wants $198,000.
A. David prevails and owes nothing because he acted in good faith and substantially performed.
B. Goliath gets $198,000 because David failed to perform an express term, regardless of intent.
C. Goliath prevails on breach, but should recover about $6,000 because David substantially performed, the breach was inadvertent, and replacement cost is grossly disproportionate to the loss in value.
D. David materially breached, but Goliath recovers only $6,000 because construction-defect damages are always limited to diminution in value.
(Answer slide not captured. Per Jacob & Youngs / R348(2): **C**. Good faith doesn't erase the breach; "always" in D is wrong.)

## The Garage (repudiation: conditional refusal over a "minor" term)
Jolie contracts (Aug 1) to sell Jack Construction her 6-car garage; deed and $100,000 due Sept 30 at the Law Offices of Cecil B. Moore; the contract says each party may bring only counsel, and no pets, firearms or illegal substances are permitted in the office. Three days before closing Jolie writes: my puppy just had surgery; unless you agree I can bring him to closing, I will not appear and deliver the deed on Sept 30. Has Jolie repudiated?
**Answer: Yes.** It's not merely a request to modify a minor term; it's a definite, conditional refusal to perform a material obligation unless Jack agrees to a modification he's not obligated to accept. That the concession seems minor doesn't matter; what matters is the consequence attached (no appearance, no deed), which goes to the heart of the deal.
- Key distinction: "Could we change the rule so I can bring my puppy?" = request for modification, NOT repudiation. "Unless you let me bring my puppy, I will not appear or deliver the deed." = repudiation.

## Rivera (repudiation + insufficient retraction; Truman L. Flatt)
On March 1, Rivera Manufacturing contracts to produce and deliver 50,000 custom glass bottles to Sterling Foods on June 1 for $250,000; the bottles are specially designed and can't readily be sold elsewhere. May 10, Rivera writes: "Unless Sterling agrees to increase the contract price to $310,000, we will not manufacture or deliver the bottles on June 1. We would prefer to continue doing business with you, but we will not perform at the current price." May 12: "We remain willing to discuss the matter and hope the parties can reach an agreement." Which answer is most accurate?
A. No repudiation; May 10 merely proposed a modification. B. Repudiated May 10 but effectively retracted May 12 by expressing willingness to negotiate. C. Repudiated May 10 by unequivocally conditioning performance on a new term, and May 12 didn't retract it. D. No repudiation; Sterling must wait until June 1.
**Answer: C.** A statement that, fairly read, shows an intention not to perform except on conditions beyond the contract is a repudiation. May 12 doesn't clearly withdraw the refusal. In Truman, the letter retracted because it clearly and unambiguously indicated an intent to perform; a repudiating party may retract before the other side materially changes position or treats it as final. No clear message to perform on May 12.

## Coach Momo Analysis (R253: no anticipatory claim when only payment remains)
(Fact slide not captured: Momo delivered a Range Rover on June 1; Ralph owes $73,000 on June 15; Ralph's June 2 angry letter refuses to pay; Momo sues June 6.)
**Answer: Coach Momo must wait until June 15 to sue.** Ralph's June 2 letter is definite enough to be a repudiation, but under R253 a repudiation doesn't give an immediate total-breach claim when the repudiating party has already received the entire agreed exchange for its promise to pay. Momo fully performed June 1; Ralph's only remaining duty is to pay on June 15. If Ralph doesn't pay then, Momo may sue. That Ralph is known for outrageous statements when angry doesn't change the result; the limit comes from R253, not uncertainty about his meaning. ## Tom Wood (impracticability: substitute goods available)

## The Restaurant (R241, R242: minor breach, timely offer to cure)
ABC hired IMB Construction to renovate a restaurant for $600,000: start by April 1, finish by May 25, so it could open June 5. No one from IMB showed up April 1. On April 2 at 8 am, CEO Harry called VP Lisa Moon, admitted he "dropped the ball" because his assistant was in a minor car accident and hospitalized for observation, and said IMB would add 2 laborers and work overtime to finish early, by April 20. ABC wasn't injured and the work could still be done, but Lisa lost confidence, didn't want her first task as VP to be a "bust," told Harry they breached and ABC wouldn't pay or use them, and barred them from the property. IMB sues.
A. IMB likely prevails because its CEO apologized and timely offered to cure. B. IMB likely prevails because it only missed 1 work day. C. ABC likely prevails because IMB breached. D. ABC likely prevails because Moon isn't obligated to excuse or waive IMB's material breach.
**Answer: A.** Under R241 & R242, the 1-day delay wasn't a material or total breach: IMB promptly offered to cure and could still finish early; ABC suffered no harm, so termination was wrongful. B: the number of days isn't decisive; the timely offer to cure and lack of harm are. C: not every breach excuses performance; at most a minor breach, promptly cured. D: not a total breach, so Moon couldn't treat the contract as discharged.

## Raymond v. Lisa (willful abandonment defeats substantial performance)
Raymond hired Lisa's Contracting to build a house to specifications for $300K. Mid-construction, Lisa wins a lucrative $4M hospital contract that must start in 24 hours, stops work at 80-85% completion, and leaves the flooring, painting, heating, AC, doors and cabinets unfinished. Raymond pays nothing. Lisa claims substantial performance.
**Answer: Raymond prevails.** Essential items were left undone, making the house fundamentally uninhabitable, and the breach was willful (the hospital job). Jacob & Youngs: willful abandonment defeats substantial performance. Raymond may withhold payment (material breach) and recover the cost of completion by another contractor. Lisa isn't entitled to the contract price; she might claim the reasonable value of work performed to prevent unjust enrichment, but not under substantial performance.

## Joan's Carpet: Jones's analysis (R226)
(Facts as in TWEN.) R226: a condition need not be express; it can be implied from the agreement, the circumstances, or by law. In landlord-tenant repair clauses, courts almost always treat reasonable notice by the tenant as an implied condition precedent to the landlord's duty to repair. Without notice the landlord can't perform, so no duty to pay for resulting damage arises. Applied: the lease was silent, but the court implies Joan had to timely notify; she delayed April 29 to May 5, so the carpet was ruined before Landlord could act; Landlord repaired immediately on notice. **The implied condition wasn't satisfied, so the landlord's duty to cover the carpet never arose. Landlord prevails.**

## enXco Development Corp. v. Northern States: Minimum Learning Objectives (R224-230, 237, 239, 245)
- Know what language creates an express condition. No magic words, but language must unmistakably make performance conditional: "if," "when," "provided that," "unless," "until."
- Express conditions ordinarily require STRICT compliance; if one doesn't occur, the conditioned duty doesn't become due unless the condition is waived or excused.
- Issue: Was NSP required to close when enXco failed to satisfy the express condition precedent to obtain the final, nonappealable permit by the Long-Stop Date? (No.)
- Rule (R224): a condition is an event, not certain to occur, that must occur before performance becomes due, unless its nonoccurrence is excused.
- Doctrine of Prevention (R245): when a party's breach by nonperformance contributes materially to the nonoccurrence of a condition of that party's own duty, the nonoccurrence is excused.

## Lenawee County Board of Health v. Messerly: Minimum Learning Objectives (R152, 154)
- Issue: can a mutual mistake about habitability (defective septic system) justify rescinding a purchase contract with an "as is" clause?
- A mistake is a belief not in accord with the facts, about a fact existing when the contract is made. Not voidable when the adversely affected party bears the risk, including by an "as is" clause.
- **Mutual mistake, R152 & R154 = "BEN":** B = Basic assumption on which the contract was made; E = material Effect on the agreed exchange; N = adversely affected party does Not bear the risk.
- Lenawee: both were mistaken, but the purchasers assumed the risk through the "as is" clause ("Purchaser has examined this property and agrees to accept same in its present condition. There are no other or additional written or oral understandings."), so rescission was unavailable.
