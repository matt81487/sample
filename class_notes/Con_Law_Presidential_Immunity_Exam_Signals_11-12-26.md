# Con Law, class 11/12/26: Executive power, privilege, and presidential immunity

Source: `Con_Law_Class_Notes_11-12-26_Presidential_Immunity.docx` (the student's own notes). Items marked **[background]** don't come from the notes. They're added context to check against the reader before relying on them.

## What the notes say

1. **Executive privilege yields.** Presidential privilege gives way when evidence is needed in a criminal prosecution. This ties back to *United States v. Nixon* (1974) from Week 11.
2. **The question for the unit:** is the President immune from civil or criminal liability?
3. **Article II and the single executive.** Executive power is vested in one person, to supply "energy" and "vigor" to get the job done (Hamilton). The notes cite Fed. 77 for "a single person rather than a community."
   - **[background]** Hamilton's "energy in the executive" and unity argument is Federalist No. 70.
4. **Potential liability.** The President could be impeached and then prosecuted afterward. The notes cite Fed. 77.
   - **[background]** The Week 10 notes and outline put this in Federalist No. 69: the President can be impeached, removed, and then prosecuted like anyone else. Federalist No. 77 (Week 11) is about the Senate's role in appointing and "displacing" officers. Check which number the professor meant.
5. **Tench Coxe** (assistant to Hamilton at Treasury) wrote that the President's "person is not so much protected as that of a member of the House of Representatives; for he may be proceeded against like any other man in the ordinary course of law."
   - **[background]** The comparison works because Article I, §6 gives members of Congress a privilege from arrest during sessions and Speech or Debate immunity. Article II gives the President no express immunity.
6. **Ulysses S. Grant (President 1869–1877) was arrested.** This is the historical example that a sitting President isn't above ordinary legal process.
   - **[background]** The usual account: in 1872 a D.C. police officer stopped Grant for speeding in his horse-drawn carriage, and Grant paid the fine.

## Likely MC angles (built only from the points above)

- **Privilege vs. immunity:** executive privilege protects communications and yields to a criminal trial's specific need (*Nixon*). Immunity is about the President's own liability, a separate question.
- **Founding-era evidence AGAINST broad immunity:**
  - The text of Article II has no immunity clause, while Article I, §6 protects legislators.
  - Hamilton said the President can be impeached and then prosecuted.
  - Coxe said the President is "proceeded against like any other man."
  - Grant's arrest.
- **Founding-era evidence FOR broad presidential independence:** the unitary executive, vested in one person for "energy" and "vigor."
- **Trap answers:**
  - "The Constitution expressly grants the President immunity." It doesn't.
  - "The President has the same Speech or Debate protection as Congress." That clause is in Article I only.
  - "Impeachment is the only remedy, so prosecution is barred forever." The founding view was impeachment, then prosecution.
  - "Privilege is absolute." It yields in criminal cases.
