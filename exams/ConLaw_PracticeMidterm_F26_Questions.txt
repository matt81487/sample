3.   Federal patent and copyright laws are authorized by an enumerated power specifically
     about patent and copyright law, what the Supreme Court called the Intellectual Property
     Clause in Allen v. Cooper (2020). Federal trademark law, by contrast, is not authorized by
     a specific power directed at trademarks. Instead, federal trademark law is based on the
     Commerce Clause in Article I, section 8. Assume that in 2026 Congress authorizes suits for
     money damages against states for trademark infringement. The statute explicitly states it is
     based on Congress’ powers under the Commerce Clause. The statute explicitly waives state
     sovereign immunity. Is this waiver of sovereign immunity constitutional?

     A. Yes. Unlike the statute in Allen, this statute is based on the Commerce Clause, not the
        Intellectual Property Clause. While Congress cannot abrogate state sovereign immunity
        under the Intellectual Property Clause in Article I, section 8, Congress can abrogate
        sovereign immunity under the Commerce Clause.

     B. Yes. This waiver of sovereign immunity is based on the plan of the Convention.

     C. No. Congress cannot abrogate state sovereign immunity under the Commerce Clause.

     D. No. While states can voluntarily waive sovereign immunity, Congress can never
        abrogate state sovereign immunity.

11.   Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act of 1978, 50 U.S.C. §1881a, allows
      the Attorney General and the Director of National Intelligence to acquire foreign
      intelligence information by jointly authorizing the surveillance of individuals who are not
      “United States persons” and who are reasonably believed to be located outside the United
      States. Before doing so, the Attorney General and the Director of National Intelligence
      normally must obtain the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court’s approval.

      Assume the plaintiffs are attorneys whose work requires them to engage in sensitive
      international communications with individuals who they believe are likely targets of
      surveillance under §1881a. They claim that the risk of surveillance is requiring them to take
      costly and burdensome measures to protect the confidentiality of their communications.

      The plaintiffs seek a declaration that §1881a violates the Fourth Amendment’s prohibition
      on unreasonable searches, as well as an injunction against surveillance under §1881a. The
      plaintiffs do not challenge other statutory provisions that could allow for surveillance of
      their communications.

      Do the plaintiffs have Article III standing to seek this prospective relief?

      A. No. Because the government must seek the approval of the Foreign Intelligence
         Surveillance Court to engage in surveillance, there is no violation of the Fourth
         Amendment.

      B. No. The plaintiffs cannot show that their communications have been or will be
         intercepted; therefore, the plaintiffs cannot show an injury in fact. Even if the plaintiffs
         could show an actual or imminent injury from government surveillance, they cannot
         show it is traceable to section 1881a because the surveillance might be based on
         another statutory provision.

      C. Yes. Because the plaintiffs are attorneys and there is a need to protect confidential
         attorney-client communications, the plaintiffs have standing.

      D. Yes. Because the plaintiffs claim that the risk of surveillance is requiring them to take
         measures to protect the confidentiality of their communications, the plaintiffs can show
         an actual injury, which suffices to confer standing.

13.   In Marbury v. Madison (1803), could Chief Justice Marshall have offered a plausible
      interpretation of the reference to writs of mandamus in section 13 of the Judiciary Act of
      1789 that would have avoided a conflict with the Constitution?

      A. Yes, the reference to mandamus in section 13 is plausibly understood not as conferring
         jurisdiction on the Supreme Court, but as authorizing the Court to issue writs of
         mandamus in cases properly before it whenever issuing the writ is warranted by the
         principles and usages of law.

      B. Yes, the reference to mandamus in section 13 is plausibly understood as authorizing the
         district courts and circuits courts to issue writs of mandamus, not the Supreme Court.

      C. Yes, the reference to mandamus in section 13 is plausibly understood as denying the
         Supreme Court the power to issue writs of mandamus, not as granting the Supreme
         Court the power to issue them.

      D. No, the text of section 13 is very clear: it assigned cases involving ambassadors,
         consuls, vice consuls, and cases where a plaintiff seeks a writ of prohibition or
         mandamus to the exclusive, original jurisdiction of the Supreme Court.

14.   Assume that on March 1, 2024, Smith and several other individuals were distributing
      handbills (i.e., flyers or leaflets) protesting American foreign policy. They were doing so
      on an exterior sidewalk of the Woodfield Mall in Illinois. Shopping center employees
      asked them to leave. They declined to do so, and police officers were summoned. The
      officers told them they would be arrested if they did not stop handbilling. The group then
      left to avoid arrest.

      Two days later Smith and a companion returned to the shopping center and again began
      handbilling. The manager of the center called the police, and Smith and his companion
      were once again told that failure to stop their handbilling would result in their arrests.
      Smith left to avoid arrest, but his companion stayed and continued handbilling. This
      companion was arrested and subsequently arraigned on a charge of criminal trespass in
      violation of state law.

      Smith filed a complaint against the local chief of police and the owner of the Woodfield
      Mall. He alleged in his complaint that, although he desired to return to the shopping center
      to distribute handbills, he had not done so because of his concern that he would also be
      arrested. The parties stipulated that, if Smith returned and refused to stop handbilling, he
      might be arrested and charged with a violation of the state criminal trespass statute.

      Smith claims the enforcement of the statute against him violates his First and Fourteenth
      Amendment rights of freedom of speech. He seeks an injunction prohibiting the
      enforcement of the statute so as to interfere with what he claims are constitutionally
      protected activities.

      Can Smith show an injury in fact sufficient to show Article III standing?

      A. Yes. Because there is a credible threat of prosecution, Smith can show a sufficiently
         imminent injury that is concrete and particularized. Therefore, Smith can show an
         injury in fact sufficient for standing.

      B. No. Because it is not certain that Smith will be arrested, the plaintiff cannot show an
         actual or imminent injury. The parties only stipulated that he “might” be arrested. No
         actual or imminent harm, no standing.

      C. No. Because Smith cannot show a close historical or common-law analogue for his
         asserted injury, he cannot show a concrete injury. No concrete harm, no standing.

      D. No. Because the police might arrest anyone who distributes handbills at the shopping
         mall, Smith cannot show a particularized injury. No particularized harm, no standing.

19.   Assume Senator John Gillnitz recently watched the episode of 21 Jump Street entitled “The
      Next Generation,” which originally aired on June 7, 1987. In this episode, a high school
      student follows in the footsteps of his father and becomes a loan shark. He loans money to
      students and teachers at unlawful interest rates and then resorts to violence when they fail
      to pay on time. This episode gives Gillnitz an idea for what Jeffrey Rosen called Congress’
      “biannual Elizabethan ritual of making new federal crimes out of activities that are already
      criminal under state law.” Gillnitz sponsors a bill to make loan sharking in schools a
      federal crime. Congress does not hold any hearings or gather any evidence on the
      prevalence of loan sharking by students or how common it is in schools. Nevertheless, the
      law passes both houses of Congress and is signed by President Trump.

      After the law goes into effect, a high school student in Texas is prosecuted in federal court
      for violating the law. The student challenges the constitutionality of the law based on the
      Supreme Court’s holding in Lopez v. United States (1995). Will the student’s constitutional
      challenge succeed?

      A. His challenge is not likely to succeed. Congress can regulate criminal activity in or near
         schools because these crimes significantly undermine the quality of education in our
         nation’s classrooms, and education has an effect on interstate and foreign commerce.

      B. His challenge is not likely to succeed. The Court previously upheld a federal criminal
         law on loan sharking even when applied to purely intrastate transactions. The Court did
         not question this holding in Lopez.

      C. His challenge is likely to succeed. While the Court probably would have upheld the law
         prior to the decision in Lopez, Lopez requires Congress to make particularized findings
         and Congress failed to do so when it passed this bill.

      D. His challenge is likely to succeed. The regulation of activities within schools is within
         the states’ reserved powers under the Tenth Amendment. Congress should have instead
         conditioned federal funding for schools on state’s criminalizing loan sharking instead of
         regulating this activity directly.
